# Copywriting

## When to use
- New copy for a landing page, home page, feature page, pricing page, ad, email, or in-app message.
- Existing copy needs a rewrite rather than a polish. For tightening copy that is mostly right, use
  copy-editing instead.

## Gather first
Get these before drafting. Read the product, site, and repo first and ask only for what is missing.
- **Reader**: who lands here, what they were doing just before, and how aware they are of the problem
  and of this product.
- **Job**: the outcome they want, in their words. Reviews, support tickets, and sales notes are the best
  source of real phrasing.
- **Offer**: what the product actually does, what it costs, and what makes it different from the
  realistic alternative (often a spreadsheet, a manual process, or doing nothing).
- **Proof available**: customers, numbers the user can stand behind, demos, guarantees, credentials.
- **Action**: the single thing this page should get the reader to do.
- **Voice constraints**: brand tone, words to avoid, legal or regulatory limits.
If proof or numbers are missing, leave clearly marked placeholders. Never invent testimonials,
statistics, customer logos, or awards.

## Procedure

### 1. Write the promise first
State, in one sentence, what the reader gets and why they should believe it. Everything else on the
page supports this sentence. If you cannot write it, the positioning is unclear; raise that with the
user instead of hiding it under adjectives.

### 2. Match the reader's awareness
- Unaware of the problem: open with the situation or cost they recognise.
- Aware of the problem, not of solutions: name the problem and show a better way.
- Comparing solutions: lead with the difference and evidence.
- Already know the product: lead with the offer and remove friction to act.

### 3. Draft the structure
A typical landing page flows like this, but cut whatever this reader does not need:
- Headline (the promise) and a subhead that adds the how or for-whom.
- Primary call to action, visible without scrolling.
- Proof close to the claim it supports.
- The two to four benefits that matter most, each tied to a concrete feature or mechanism.
- How it works, in a few steps, if the mechanism is not obvious.
- Objections answered directly: price, effort to switch, risk, trust, "will it work for me".
- A closing call to action that restates the outcome.

### 4. Write in specifics
- Concrete beats clever: "Send invoices in two clicks" over "Effortless invoicing".
- Use the customer's vocabulary, not internal jargon.
- One idea per sentence. Short paragraphs. Front-load the important words.
- Benefits answer "so what?"; keep asking until you reach something the reader cares about.
- CTA buttons say what happens next ("Start free trial", "See pricing"), not "Submit".

### 5. Produce options
Give at least three distinct headline/subhead directions, each from a different angle (outcome, pain,
differentiation, audience call-out, proof-led), with a one-line rationale. Then write the full page for
the strongest direction, and note which elements are worth A/B testing.

### 6. Check
- Read it as a sceptical reader from the defined audience. Where would they stop believing?
- Every claim is true and supportable. Flag anything that needs legal review (health, finance,
  guarantees, comparative claims naming competitors).
- Scan for vague filler: "seamless", "powerful", "revolutionary", "best-in-class", "unlock".
- If the copy goes into code, keep it in the existing content/i18n files, respect length limits of the
  layout, and render the page to check it fits on mobile.

## Pitfalls
- Writing about the company instead of the reader.
- Several promises competing on one page.
- Hiding the price or the action behind clever copy.
- Fabricated urgency or scarcity.
- Copy that only works at desktop width.

## Deliver
Lead with the recommended headline and subhead. Then the alternatives with rationale, the full draft
in page order, a list of placeholders the user must fill with real proof, and suggested tests. If you
edited files, say which and whether you viewed the rendered result. Never publish or send copy without
the user's approval.
