# Competitor Analysis

## When to use
- Building a landscape before positioning or a launch.
- A sales team needs a battlecard against a specific rival.
- Writing a public "us vs them" or "alternatives to X" page.

When not to use: the request needs non-public information (leaked decks, posing as a customer to extract pricing, scraping behind logins against terms). Decline that part and stick to public sources.

## Gather first
- Our product: what it does, who it is for, pricing, real strengths and real gaps. If a product context document exists in the repo or docs, read it first.
- Which competitors, or how to find them: direct (same job, same buyer), indirect (different approach to the same job), and the status quo (spreadsheets, an agency, doing nothing).
- The output wanted and its audience: internal strategy, sales, or public page. Public pages carry legal and reputational risk; internal ones can be blunter.

## Procedure
1. **Build the list.** Search for the problem in the buyer's words, category terms, "alternatives to <known player>", review-site categories, and app marketplaces. Ask the user which names come up in lost deals; that list beats any search.
2. **Tier them.** Pick three to five to profile deeply; list the rest in one line each. Depth on the few that actually show up in deals is worth more than breadth.
3. **Profile each one from primary sources** (their site, pricing page, docs, changelog, status page, job posts, public filings, founder posts) and secondary ones (reviews, community threads, analyst notes, press). For each, capture:
   - Who they target and the promise in their headline.
   - Packaging and price, including what is gated behind sales calls.
   - Core capabilities and notable absences (check the docs, not just marketing).
   - Signals of direction: recent launches, hiring areas, funding, acquisitions.
   - What customers praise and complain about, quoted in their words.
4. **Cite and date everything.** Every claim gets a source link and the date you saw it. Pricing and features change; undated comparisons rot into falsehoods. Mark inferences as inferences.
5. **Compare on dimensions buyers use to decide**, not on a feature checklist that happens to favour us. Ask: what does a buyer weigh when choosing? (time to value, total cost, integrations, compliance, support, switching cost).
6. **Be honest about where we lose.** Every competitor wins somewhere. Naming it builds credibility and tells sales which deals to qualify out.
7. **Synthesise.** Find the open space: needs nobody serves well, segments everyone ignores, claims nobody can credibly make but us. That is the input to positioning.

## Output formats
**Landscape (internal):** a short summary of the market and the openings, a comparison table, and one section per profiled competitor. Include a "what would change our view" note.

**Battlecard (sales):** one page per competitor. Contents: when we meet them; their pitch in one line; where they are strong (concede it); where we are stronger, each backed by proof; landmines to plant (questions the buyer should ask that expose their gaps, phrased fairly); common objections with short responses; signs this deal is a bad fit for us. Keep it skimmable mid-call.

**Comparison page (public):**
- State the date of comparison and link sources.
- Represent the competitor accurately enough that their own customer would agree.
- Include rows where they win and say who they suit better.
- No disparagement, no unverifiable superlatives, no use of their trademarks beyond fair naming. Comparative advertising rules vary by country; for anything legally sensitive, have a qualified person review it. This skill is not legal advice.
- Give the page an owner and a review cadence so it does not go stale.

## Pitfalls
- Treating a competitor's marketing claim as a verified capability.
- Confusing "they have a page about it" with "customers use it".
- Profiling twenty companies shallowly.
- Ignoring the status quo, which is often the biggest competitor.
- Writing a battlecard so hostile that reps lose credibility using it.

## Deliver
Lead with the two or three findings that should change a decision. Then the requested artefact. State what was verified directly (pages read, dates), what was inferred, and what could not be checked (for example enterprise pricing behind a sales call). Ask before publishing any public comparison page.
