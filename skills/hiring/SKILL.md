# Hiring

Good hiring starts by defining what success in the role looks like, then measures every candidate against that same definition. Most hiring mistakes come from skipping the first step.

**This is not legal or HR advice.** Employment law differs by country and region (discrimination, pay transparency, background checks, contracts, immigration). Have a qualified HR professional or employment lawyer review offer letters, contracts, and screening practices, particularly for first hires in a jurisdiction. Never send a job post, rejection, or offer, or contact candidates, without explicit user approval.

## When to use (and not)

Use for designing a role, writing the post, setting screening criteria, planning interviews, and drafting an offer. Not for terminations, disciplinary matters, immigration sponsorship decisions, or disputes; involve a professional.

## What to gather first

- Why the role exists now and what problem it solves.
- Team, manager, location or remote policy, employment type, start date.
- Salary range and benefits (needed for the post in many places and for honest conversations everywhere).
- Must-have constraints: licences, language, time zone, legal right to work.
- Any existing job descriptions, careers page, or brand voice to match.

## Procedure

### 1. Scorecard
Write before anything else:
- **Mission**: one or two sentences on why the role exists.
- **Outcomes**: three to five measurable results expected in the first year, e.g. "reduce invoice processing time so month-end closes within five working days".
- **Competencies**: the skills and behaviours needed to deliver those outcomes, each with what "strong" looks like. Separate must-have from trainable.
Remove requirements that do not trace to an outcome, especially degree or years-of-experience filters that exclude capable people without predicting performance.

### 2. Job post
- Lead with what the person will do and achieve, not company boilerplate.
- Include salary range, location/remote terms, and the hiring steps with an approximate timeline.
- List must-haves briefly; mark nice-to-haves clearly so people do not self-reject.
- Use inclusive, plain language: avoid gendered words, age-coded phrases ("digital native"), and unnecessary physical requirements.
- Say how to apply and what to include.

### 3. Screening
- Define screening criteria from the scorecard before reading applications, and apply them consistently.
- For each applicant, record evidence against each must-have: met, partly, not evident. Advance on evidence, not on prestige of employer or school.
- Do not infer or score protected characteristics (age, gender, ethnicity, religion, disability, family status, and so on); ignore photos and personal details irrelevant to the job.
- If you are given applications to screen, produce a table with evidence quotes and a recommendation, and note borderline cases for a human decision. The final decision belongs to the user.

### 4. Structured interview plan
- Map each competency to one interview stage, so nothing is assessed twice and nothing is skipped.
- For each stage: purpose, interviewer, duration, questions, and a rubric.
- Use behavioural questions ("tell me about a time you…") with follow-ups probing the candidate's own actions and the result, and situational or work-sample tasks that resemble the real job. Keep take-home work short and respect candidates' time.
- Write a scoring rubric with anchors for each level (e.g. 1 to 4) describing what an answer at that level contains.
- Every candidate gets the same core questions. Interviewers score independently before any debrief.
- List questions that must never be asked (about family plans, religion, age, health, and other protected areas).

### 5. Offer letter draft
Include: title, start date, manager, compensation and pay frequency, benefits summary, employment type, location/remote terms, probation if any, contingencies (references, right-to-work checks), acceptance deadline, and who to contact. Keep the tone warm and clear. Mark the draft as requiring review against local law and the company's employment contract; offer letters can create binding terms.

## Pitfalls

- Hiring for "culture fit" without defining it; use defined values with observable behaviours instead.
- Changing the bar mid-process for a candidate interviewers liked.
- Unrealistic requirement lists that describe three jobs.
- Making promises in the offer (bonus, equity, promotion timing) the user has not confirmed.

## Deliverable

Deliver whichever pieces were asked for, in order: scorecard, job post, screening criteria (and screening table if applications were provided), interview plan with rubrics, offer letter draft. Lead with a short note on any open decisions (e.g. salary range missing). End by listing assumptions, items needing HR or legal review, and a reminder that nothing has been sent.
