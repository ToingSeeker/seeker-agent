# Brand Identity

A brand is the consistent impression a product leaves: what it stands for, how it sounds, and how it looks. This skill captures those decisions in a compact guide and checks materials against them.

## When to use

- A new product or company needs a voice and basic visual identity.
- Existing materials feel inconsistent and need a reference.
- Reviewing copy, a page, or a campaign for brand fit.

Not for drawing a final logo (recommend a designer for that; you can brief one), trademark clearance, or legal review of claims. Trademark and advertising-claim questions are legal matters: say so and suggest a qualified professional.

## Gather first

1. What the product does, for whom, and the main alternative customers use today.
2. Existing assets: logo files, colours, fonts, website, past campaigns. Search the repo for brand folders, theme files and copy.
3. Ambitions and limits: words the founders want associated, words to avoid, tone in sensitive moments.
4. Markets and languages, since voice and imagery must work in each.

If the user has none of this, run a short interview (five or six questions at most) instead of guessing.

## Procedure

### 1. Positioning
Write one short statement covering: audience, their problem, what the product is, the key difference from alternatives, and the reason to believe it. Keep it internal; it is a filter, not a tagline.

### 2. Personality and voice
- Pick three or four personality traits, each with a "this, not that" boundary (e.g. confident, not arrogant). The boundary is what makes a trait usable.
- Describe tone shifts by situation: marketing, onboarding, errors, billing, apologies. The voice stays; the tone adapts.
- Give vocabulary guidance: preferred terms, banned terms, how to refer to users and to the product.
- Show two or three short rewrites demonstrating the voice on real lines from the product.

### 3. Messaging pillars
Three to four themes the brand returns to, each with a one-line claim, supporting proof points (features, facts the user can substantiate), and the audience it matters to most. Do not invent proof: mark any claim that needs evidence.

### 4. Visual identity basics
- **Logo use**: versions (full, mark only, mono), clear space, minimum size, backgrounds it works on, and misuses to avoid.
- **Colour**: primary, secondary and neutrals with hex/RGB values, the role of each, and accessible text pairings.
- **Typography**: families for display and text, with fallbacks and licence notes.
- **Imagery**: photography or illustration style, subjects, lighting, what to avoid.
- **Graphic elements**: shapes, patterns, iconography style.
Hand off implementation to `design-system` for tokens.

### 5. Write the guide
A single concise document (a page or two is often enough to be read and used). Put it where the team will find it, after asking where.

### 6. Consistency review
When reviewing materials, check each against the guide: positioning (is the main message the one we chose?), voice and terminology, claims (substantiated?), visual use (logo, colour, type, imagery), and accessibility of colour pairs. Report each issue with location, the guideline it breaks, and a suggested fix. Distinguish real inconsistency from acceptable tone variation.

## Pitfalls

- Traits so generic they exclude nothing ("innovative, friendly, trusted").
- Guidelines longer than anyone will read.
- Claims like "fastest" or "#1" without evidence, which can create legal exposure.
- Copying a competitor's identity so closely it causes confusion.
- Colours that look good on a slide but fail contrast in the product.

## Deliver

Lead with the positioning statement and voice summary, then the guide (or review findings). List any claims needing evidence and any decisions that remain the user's (name changes, logo direction, legal wording). Say which existing materials you actually reviewed.
