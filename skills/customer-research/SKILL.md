# Customer Research

## When to use
- Before positioning, messaging, pricing or a roadmap bet, when the team is arguing from opinion.
- When a pile of raw material (transcripts, tickets, reviews, survey answers) needs turning into findings.

When not to use: the question is quantitative and the data already exists in analytics (query it instead); or the decision is already made and research is wanted only as cover (say so).

## Gather first
- The decision this research informs and the deadline. Research without a decision drifts.
- What is already known: past studies, analytics, support tags, sales notes.
- Who the target respondents are and how to reach them (customer list, churned users, waitlist, communities).
- Constraints: consent, privacy rules, incentives budget, languages.

## Choose the method
- **Interviews**: to learn why, in what context, and in what words. Five to ten per segment usually surfaces the main patterns; stop when new interviews stop adding themes.
- **Surveys**: to size something you already understand qualitatively. Bad at discovering new ideas.
- **Existing-text mining**: reviews (yours and competitors'), support tickets, sales call notes, forum and community threads, churn reasons. Cheap, fast, unprompted - start here.
- **Observation / usability sessions**: to see what people do rather than what they say.

## Procedure
1. **Write the research questions** (what we need to learn), separate from the interview questions (what we ask). Keep to three to five.
2. **Mine existing text first.** Collect into one file with source and date per item. Read for recurring complaints, triggers, workarounds, comparisons, and vivid phrases. Count roughly how often each theme appears, but treat counts as signals, not statistics.
3. **Recruit.** Mix recent buyers, long-time users, churned users and people who evaluated and chose something else. Screen for the behaviour you care about, not demographics alone. Do not interview only fans.
4. **Write the interview guide.** Anchor on a specific past event ("tell me about the last time you..."), not hypotheticals ("would you use..."). Probe the timeline: what triggered the search, what they tried, what almost stopped them, what success looks like now. Avoid leading questions and pitching. Leave room for silence.
5. **Write the survey** if needed: one goal, short, neutral wording, one idea per question, balanced scales, an "other / not sure" option, open text for the most important question. Pilot it with two or three people before sending.
6. **Run and record** with consent. Take notes verbatim where possible; the exact words are the asset.
7. **Synthesise.**
   - Break notes into atomic observations, each tagged with participant and source.
   - Cluster bottom-up into themes; name each theme with a claim, not a topic ("setup takes a developer, so marketers stall" rather than "onboarding").
   - For each theme record: how many participants, strongest quotes, counter-evidence.
   - Pull out: the jobs customers hire the product for; the triggering moments; pains and their severity; the alternatives they compare against; desired outcomes; and the vocabulary they use.
   - Separate segments if they behave differently; averaged personas hide the signal.
8. **Check rigour**: did one loud participant drive a theme? Are you reporting what people did or what they predicted? What would disconfirm each finding?

## Pitfalls
- Asking customers to design the solution.
- Treating stated willingness to pay as real.
- Recruiting only from the easiest list (power users).
- Over-trusting survey percentages from small or self-selected samples.
- Storing personal data longer or more widely than consent allows; anonymise quotes before sharing.

## Deliver
- A findings document leading with the three to five insights that bear on the decision, each with evidence strength (how many sources, which types) and a recommended action.
- A customer-language bank: verbatim phrases grouped by theme, ready for copywriting.
- Appendix: method, sample (counts by segment), guide or survey used, raw tagged notes.
- State plainly what the sample can and cannot support and which questions remain open.
